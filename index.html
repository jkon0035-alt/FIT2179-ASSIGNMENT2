Chapter One — Tobacco Smoking

A Decades-Long Decline

Australia's anti-smoking policies — plain packaging in 2012, regular excise increases, and comprehensive smoke-free laws — have driven smoking to historic lows. In 2001, nearly one in four Australians smoked daily. By 2022–23, fewer than one in ten did. Yet remote communities and disadvantaged groups remain stubbornly entrenched.

50–59 Highest-smoking age group

This cohort began smoking in the 1980s and 90s — before comprehensive advertising bans. Nicotine dependence at this stage is significantly harder to break, making them the most stubborn remaining group despite decades of public health messaging.

Why the gender gap is closing

Men have historically smoked at higher rates, but the decline has been proportionally faster among males — the 3.0 point gap in 2001 has compressed to just 1.3 points. Policies like plain packaging and excise increases appear to have reached both genders almost equally.

The youngest adults have already moved on

Australians aged 18–24 now smoke at just 6.9% — the lowest of any adult age group. The question is whether vaping will hold that record, or simply delay it.

🚬

Disadvantage drives persistent smoking

Australians in the most disadvantaged quintile smoke at more than three times the rate of the least disadvantaged (20.1% vs 6.4%). Plain packaging and excise increases have disproportionately benefited higher-income groups, leaving a stubborn tail in communities with fewer quit resources and higher chronic stress.

Stream width = share of population in each category. The dominant expansion of the "Never Smoked" stream and collapse of "Smoke Daily" tell a 22-year story at a glance. A dashed line marks the 2012 plain packaging inflection. Click a legend item to isolate a stream.

The big picture in one chart: The "Never Smoked" stream has grown from 50.6% in 2001 to 65.4% in 2023 — two-thirds of Australians today have no experience with cigarettes at all. The "Smoked Previously" stream has stayed flat, meaning ex-smokers are not returning. The only streams shrinking are the two smoking categories — a clear sign of a population-level shift, not just individual choice.

Chapter Two — Vaping & E-Cigarettes

The New Nicotine Epidemic

While smoking has fallen, vaping has surged — especially among the young. In 2022–23, 18.1% of 18–24 year olds reported vaping, up from just 5.4% in 2016. Despite prescription-only laws introduced mid-2023, illicit disposables remain widely accessible. The data raises serious questions about whether vaping is creating a new generation of nicotine-dependent users.

Vaping now outpaces smoking among young adults

Among 18–24 year olds, vaping (18.1%) has overtaken smoking (10.4%) as the more common nicotine behaviour. Australia's strict prescription-only regulations have largely failed to curb youth uptake — illicit single-use disposables remain accessible through convenience stores and online channels with minimal enforcement.

Key finding — Chapter Two

Substitution, not cessation. The age groups that have cut smoking the most are precisely the ones vaping the most. The 18–24 group reduced daily smoking by 5.9 points since 2001 — the largest decline of any age group — yet now records the highest vaping rate at 20.6%. This is not a public health win. It is a substitution of one nicotine product for another, with the long-term health consequences of vaping still poorly understood.

Geography flips. NSW leads the nation in vaping yet has among the lowest smoking rates. Tasmania has the lowest vaping rate yet relatively high smoking. Urban coastal states are driving the vaping trend; remote and regional areas remain bound to traditional tobacco — and the policy response for each requires a completely different approach.

Chapter Three — Illicit Drug Use

Cannabis Rises, Cocaine Surges

Cannabis remains Australia's most widely used illicit substance at a record 12% past-year prevalence. Cocaine use has more than doubled since 2010, driven by greater supply and social normalisation in urban professional settings. Methamphetamine, after the 2013–16 "ice epidemic" spike, has stabilised but remains concentrated in specific geographic and demographic groups.

Each Drug Has a Distinct Age Profile — Cannabis Is Uniquely Broad

Each spoke = one age group. Each polygon = one drug. A wider polygon means that drug is more evenly spread across ages. Cannabis forms the largest polygon. Cocaine and ecstasy collapse to a narrow spike in the 20–29 bracket. Hover any point for the exact rate.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23. Past-year use by age group and substance.

Cannabis stands apart: Unlike every other drug shown, cannabis use does not collapse after age 30. Its polygon remains wide across all age brackets — evidence that cannabis has become normalised across generations. Cocaine and ecstasy show sharply peaked polygons in the 20–29 bracket and near-zero use beyond 40. Methamphetamine, by contrast, shows a flatter profile across 20–49 — consistent with its presence in working-age regional communities.

🌿

Cannabis at its highest ever recorded prevalence

At 12% past-year use, cannabis is more prevalent than in any previous NDSHS survey. Shifting attitudes — including ACT decriminalisation, growing medical cannabis access, and greater social acceptance — are all contributing. Young adults (18–34) account for a disproportionate share of users.

The alcohol paradox: Alcohol does not appear in the illicit drug charts because it is legal — yet the hospitalisation data makes its true cost unmistakable. At 80,439 annual admissions, alcohol causes more medical harm than cannabis, methamphetamine, opioids, and sedatives combined. Legal status and public health risk are not the same thing.

Cocaine overtakes ecstasy for the first time: For the first time in NDSHS history, cocaine has become the second most commonly used illicit drug after cannabis. Its growing share reflects a genuine increase in use — not just a decline in ecstasy — consistent with greater supply availability and changing social norms in urban professional settings.

Chapter Four — Geographic Patterns

Remoteness & Risk

Drug use is not evenly distributed across Australia. Remoteness strongly predicts tobacco, cannabis and methamphetamine use, while cocaine is concentrated in major cities. The maps below tell the story of two Australias: metropolitan areas where smoking is declining but vaping is emerging, and remote communities where tobacco remains a severe, under-resourced public health challenge.

20.4% Smoking rate in Remote/Very Remote areas

Two Australias: city and remote

In major cities, roughly 1 in 14 people smoke daily. In very remote communities, it is nearly 1 in 5. Illicit drug use follows the same gradient.

How Remoteness Shapes Substance Use Across Australia

Pin size encodes the selected rate — click the radio buttons to switch between drug use and smoking. Use the dropdown to isolate a remoteness category. Hover any pin for location, state, and exact rates.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table S3.5. ABS ASGS 2021 remoteness categories.

Key finding — Chapter Four

The remoteness gradient is consistent across both metrics. Switching the radio button from drug use to smoking shows the same pattern — Remote and Very Remote pins are larger in both views. The 20.4% smoking rate in Remote/Very Remote areas is nearly three times the 7.0% rate in Major Cities. This is not coincidence: remoteness correlates with socioeconomic disadvantage, reduced access to health services, and higher chronic stress — all established predictors of substance use.

Vaping tells the opposite story. Data from Chapter 2 shows vaping is more prevalent in cities. This creates a two-track policy challenge: urban areas have reduced smoking but face a new vaping problem, while remote communities remain bound to traditional tobacco with fewer resources to address it.

Chapter Five — State Comparison

The NT Leads Every State on All Three Substances

Breaking substance use down by state and territory reveals a clear geographic divide. The ACT consistently records the lowest prevalence of smoking and illicit drug use, while the Northern Territory leads the nation across all three substance categories. A strong positive correlation exists between smoking and drug use rates — states that smoke more also tend to use more drugs.

The NT–ACT divide: Clicking the NT bar shows it leading across all three panels — often by a wide margin. The ACT, home to a relatively affluent and educated workforce, sits at the opposite end in every panel. These are not random variations: they reflect deep structural differences in socioeconomic conditions, Indigenous population proportions, remoteness, and access to health services.

Vaping breaks the pattern: The vaping panel is notably different — NSW leads, not the NT. This confirms the finding from Chapter 2: vaping is an urban phenomenon concentrated in densely populated coastal states.

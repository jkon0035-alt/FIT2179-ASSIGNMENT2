Chapter One — Tobacco Smoking

A Decades-Long Decline

Australia's anti-smoking policies — plain packaging in 2012, regular excise increases, and comprehensive smoke-free laws — have driven smoking to historic lows. In 2001, nearly one in four Australians smoked daily. By 2022–23, fewer than one in ten did. Yet remote communities and disadvantaged groups remain stubbornly entrenched.

8.3% Daily smokers in 2022–23

One of Australia's greatest public health wins

Since 2001, a combination of price hikes, plain packaging, advertising bans, and smoke-free laws has more than halved the daily smoking rate.

The shaded area between the daily and current lines is narrowing — fewer Australians smoke only occasionally, suggesting the habit is losing its social normalcy entirely.

Each policy milestone on the chart corresponds to a visible step-down in the rate, confirming the causal link between regulation and behaviour change.

Age × Gender Heatmap

Middle-Aged Australians Smoke the Most

Daily smoking rate (%) by age group and gender. Darker cells = higher rates. The 50–59 group peaks at 12.1% for males — the darkest cell. Hover any cell for the exact rate.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Tables 2.3 & 2.4.

What the gender data tells us: Men have historically smoked at higher rates, but the decline has been proportionally faster among males — the gap of 3.0 percentage points in 2001 has compressed to just 1.3 points today. This convergence suggests that policies like plain packaging and excise increases have reached both genders almost equally.

Why middle age? The 50–59 age group smokes at the highest rate despite decades of public health messaging. This cohort began smoking in the 1980s and 1990s, before comprehensive advertising bans, and nicotine dependence at that stage is significantly harder to break. Younger Australians aged 18–24 now smoke at only 6.9% — the lowest of any adult age group.

🚬

Disadvantage drives persistent smoking

Australians in the most disadvantaged quintile smoke at more than three times the rate of the least disadvantaged (20.1% vs 6.4%). Plain packaging and excise increases have disproportionately benefited higher-income groups, leaving a stubborn tail in communities with fewer quit resources and higher chronic stress.

Streamgraph — Smoking Status Flow Over Time

How Australia's Smoking Categories Have Shifted, 2001–2023

Stream width = share of population in each category. The dominant expansion of the "Never Smoked" stream and collapse of "Smoke Daily" tell a 22-year policy success story at a glance. A dashed line marks the 2012 plain packaging inflection. Click a legend item to isolate a stream.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table 2. Estimated proportions aged 14+.

The big picture in one chart: The streamgraph makes visible what the line charts can only imply — Australia's relationship with smoking has fundamentally changed. The "Never Smoked" stream has grown from 50.6% in 2001 to 65.4% in 2023, meaning two-thirds of Australians today have no experience with cigarettes at all. Meanwhile "Smoked Previously" has remained relatively stable, suggesting that ex-smokers are not returning to the habit. The only streams shrinking are "Smoke Daily" and "Currently Smoke" — a clear sign of a population-level shift, not just individual choice.

Chapter Two — Vaping & E-Cigarettes

The New Nicotine Epidemic

While smoking has fallen, vaping has surged — especially among the young. In 2022–23, 18.1% of 18–24 year olds reported vaping, up from just 5.4% in 2016. Despite prescription-only laws introduced mid-2023, illicit disposables remain widely accessible. The data raises serious questions about whether vaping is creating a new generation of nicotine-dependent users.

Multi-Series Area Chart

Vaping by Age Group Across Three Survey Waves: 2016, 2019 & 2022–23

Each area series shows one age group across three survey years. The 18–24 group has more than tripled since 2016, reaching 20.6% — well above the 10.4% smoking rate for the same group. A dashed reference line marks that smoking rate. Hover any point for the exact rate.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table 9. Past 12-month e-cigarette use by age group.

A generational divide: Vaping is not evenly distributed — it is almost entirely concentrated in Australians under 30. The 18–24 cohort's rate of 20.6% in 2022–23 is nearly double the overall national average of 4.1%, and has tripled in just six years. By contrast, Australians aged 50 and over are barely represented in vaping statistics at all.

The crossover moment: The dashed reference line at 10.4% marks the smoking rate for 18–24 year olds. Vaping has now doubled past it. For this age group, vaping has completely displaced smoking as the dominant nicotine behaviour — raising serious questions about long-term health outcomes given that vaping's long-term effects remain poorly understood.

💨

Vaping now outpaces smoking among young adults

Among 18–24 year olds, vaping (18.1%) has overtaken smoking (10.4%) as the more common nicotine behaviour. Australia's strict prescription-only regulations have largely failed to curb youth uptake — illicit single-use disposables remain accessible through convenience stores and online channels with minimal enforcement.

Brown bars (left) = smoking decline since 2001. Teal dots (right) = 2023 vaping rate. The 18–24 group cut smoking by nearly 6 points but now leads all ages in vaping. Hover bars or dots for exact rates.

Choropleth Map

NSW Records the Highest Vaping Rate of Any State

Darker teal = higher vaping prevalence. NSW leads at 7.9%, Tasmania records the lowest at 3.7%. Hover any state for its exact rate.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table 9b. E-cigarette use past 12 months.

Substitution, not cessation: The diverging bar chart reveals a striking pattern — the age groups that have cut smoking the most are precisely the ones vaping the most. The 18–24 group reduced daily smoking by 5.9 points since 2001, the largest decline of any age group, yet now records the highest vaping rate at 20.6%. This is not a public health win — it is a substitution of one nicotine product for another.

Geography flips: The choropleth map tells a counterintuitive story. NSW, which has among the lowest smoking rates of any state, leads the nation in vaping. Tasmania, which has relatively high smoking rates, has the lowest vaping rate. Urban coastal states are driving the vaping trend, while remote and regional areas remain more strongly associated with traditional tobacco use.

Chapter Three — Illicit Drug Use

Cannabis Rises, Cocaine Surges

Cannabis remains Australia's most widely used illicit substance at a record 12% past-year prevalence. Cocaine use has more than doubled since 2010, driven by greater supply and social normalisation in urban professional settings. Methamphetamine, after the 2013–16 "ice epidemic" spike, has stabilised but remains concentrated in specific geographic and demographic groups.

Heatmap of Drug Types and Age group

Each Drug Has a Distinct Age Profile — Cannabis Is Uniquely Broad

Each spoke = one age group. Each polygon = one drug. A wider polygon means that drug is more evenly spread across ages. Cannabis forms the largest polygon. Cocaine and ecstasy collapse to a narrow spike in the 20–29 bracket. Hover any point for the exact rate.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23. Past-year use by age group and substance.

Cannabis stands apart: Unlike every other drug shown, cannabis use does not collapse after age 30. Its polygon remains wide across all age brackets — evidence that cannabis has become normalised across generations, not just among youth. This broad reach is consistent with shifting social attitudes and the expansion of medical cannabis access.

Cocaine and ecstasy are youth-concentrated: Both drugs show sharply peaked polygons in the 20–29 bracket and near-zero use beyond age 40. This reflects their association with nightlife and social settings that are specific to younger Australians. Methamphetamine, by contrast, shows a more flattened profile across the 20–49 range — consistent with its presence in regional and working-age communities.

🌿

Cannabis at its highest ever recorded prevalence

At 12% past-year use, cannabis is more prevalent than in any previous NDSHS survey. Shifting attitudes — including ACT decriminalisation, growing medical cannabis access, and greater social acceptance — are all contributing. Young adults (18–34) account for a disproportionate share of users.

Alcohol Dwarfs Every Other Substance in Hospital Burden

Dot position = annual hospitalisations. Alcohol caused 80,439 — 5.8× more than the next highest substance. Each dot is colour-coded by substance type. Hover any dot for the exact count.

Source: AIHW AODT Services 2022–23. Principal diagnosis.

The alcohol paradox: Alcohol does not appear in the illicit drug charts because it is legal — yet the hospitalisation data makes its true cost unmistakable. At 80,439 annual hospital admissions, alcohol causes more medical harm than cannabis, methamphetamine, opioids, and sedatives combined. This is a striking reminder that legal status and public health risk are not the same thing.

Cocaine's rise in the donut: For the first time in NDSHS history, cocaine has overtaken ecstasy to become the second most commonly used illicit drug after cannabis. Its growing share of the donut reflects a genuine increase in use — not just a decline in ecstasy — consistent with greater supply availability and changing social norms in urban professional circles.

Animated Bar + Year Slider

Cocaine Has Nearly Quadrupled Since 2001

Drag the slider to watch how each drug's use rate changed across survey years. From 2016 onward, a label marks the moment cocaine overtook ecstasy. Hover any bar for the exact rate.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table 5. Past-year use, persons aged 14+.

Two decades, two very different stories: Dragging the slider from 2001 to 2023 tells the story of cocaine's relentless rise alongside ecstasy's long decline. Cocaine grew from 1.3% in 2001 to 4.5% in 2023 — a 246% increase. Ecstasy peaked at 3.5% in 2007 and has since fallen to 2.1%. The crossover happened between 2013 and 2016, when cocaine first overtook ecstasy. The two drugs represent different social worlds — ecstasy is tied to rave culture, which peaked in the 2000s, while cocaine has become embedded in professional and social settings that have grown over the same period.

Chapter Four — Geographic Patterns

Remoteness & Risk

Drug use is not evenly distributed across Australia. Remoteness strongly predicts tobacco, cannabis and methamphetamine use, while cocaine is concentrated in major cities. The maps below tell the story of two Australias: metropolitan areas where smoking is declining but vaping is emerging, and remote communities where tobacco remains a severe, under-resourced public health challenge.

20.4% Smoking rate in Remote/Very Remote areas

Two Australias: city and remote

In major cities, roughly 1 in 14 people smoke daily. In very remote communities, it is nearly 1 in 5. Illicit drug use follows the same gradient — Remote and Very Remote areas consistently record higher rates across both measures.

Switch between drug use and smoking using the radio buttons to compare how the two patterns differ across the country.

Symbol Map + Radio Buttons + Remoteness Filter

How Remoteness Shapes Substance Use Across Australia

Pin size encodes the selected rate — click the radio buttons to switch between drug use and smoking. Use the dropdown to isolate a remoteness category. Hover any pin for location, state, and exact rates.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table S3.5. ABS ASGS 2021 remoteness categories.

The remoteness gradient: Switching the radio button from drug use to smoking reveals a consistent pattern — Remote and Very Remote pins are larger in both views. This is not a coincidence. Remoteness correlates with socioeconomic disadvantage, reduced access to health services, and higher rates of chronic stress, all of which are established predictors of substance use. The 20.4% smoking rate in Remote/Very Remote areas is nearly three times the 7.0% rate in Major Cities.

Where vaping fits: The vaping data from Chapter 2 tells the opposite story — vaping is more prevalent in cities. This creates a policy challenge: urban areas have successfully reduced smoking but face a new vaping problem, while remote areas remain heavily dependent on traditional tobacco with fewer resources to address it.

Chapter Five — State Comparison

The NT Leads Every State on All Three Substances

Breaking substance use down by state and territory reveals a clear geographic divide. The ACT consistently records the lowest prevalence of smoking and illicit drug use, while the Northern Territory leads the nation across all three substance categories. A strong positive correlation exists between smoking and drug use rates — states that smoke more also tend to use more drugs.

Small Multiples + Cross-Filter

Substance Use Side-by-Side Across All States & Territories

Three panels — smoking, vaping, drug use — share the same state axis. NT is labelled "highest" and ACT "lowest" in each panel. Click any bar to highlight that state across all three panels simultaneously.

Source: NDSHS 2022–23, Table 9b. Persons aged 14+.

The NT–ACT divide: No two jurisdictions better illustrate the inequality in Australia's substance use landscape than the Northern Territory and the Australian Capital Territory. Clicking the NT bar across all three panels shows it leading — often by a wide margin — in smoking, vaping, and drug use simultaneously. The ACT, home to a relatively affluent and educated workforce, sits at the opposite end in every panel. These are not random variations: they reflect deep structural differences in socioeconomic conditions, Indigenous population proportions, remoteness, and access to health services.

Vaping breaks the pattern: The vaping panel is notably different from smoking and drugs — NSW leads, not the NT. This confirms the finding from Chapter 2: vaping is an urban phenomenon, concentrated in densely populated coastal states, while the NT's substance challenges remain rooted in traditional tobacco and illicit drugs.

Chapter One — Tobacco Smoking

A Decades-Long Decline

Australia's anti-smoking policies — plain packaging in 2012, regular excise increases, and comprehensive smoke-free laws — have driven smoking to historic lows. In 2001, nearly one in four Australians smoked daily. By 2022–23, fewer than one in ten did. Yet remote communities and disadvantaged groups remain stubbornly entrenched.

One of Australia's greatest public health wins

Since 2001, a combination of price hikes, plain packaging, advertising bans, and smoke-free laws has more than halved the daily smoking rate. Every survey wave has brought a new record low.

The gap between daily and current smokers is shrinking too — fewer Australians are "social smokers" as the habit loses social normalcy.

🚬

Disadvantage drives persistent smoking

Australians in the most disadvantaged quintile smoke at more than three times the rate of the least disadvantaged (20.1% vs 6.4%). Plain packaging and excise increases have disproportionately benefited higher-income groups, leaving a stubborn tail in communities with fewer quit resources and higher chronic stress.

Chapter Two — Vaping & E-Cigarettes

The New Nicotine Epidemic

While smoking has fallen, vaping has surged — especially among the young. In 2022–23, 18.1% of 18–24 year olds reported vaping, up from just 5.4% in 2016. Despite prescription-only laws introduced mid-2023, illicit disposables remain widely accessible. The data raises serious questions about whether vaping is creating a new generation of nicotine-dependent users.

Vaping now outpaces smoking among young adults

Among 18–24 year olds, vaping (18.1%) has overtaken smoking (10.4%) as the more common nicotine behaviour. Australia's strict prescription-only regulations have largely failed to curb youth uptake — illicit single-use disposables remain accessible through convenience stores and online channels with minimal enforcement.

Chapter Three — Illicit Drug Use

Cannabis Rises, Cocaine Surges

Cannabis remains Australia's most widely used illicit substance at a record 12% past-year prevalence. Cocaine use has more than doubled since 2010, driven by greater supply and social normalisation in urban professional settings. Methamphetamine, after the 2013–16 "ice epidemic" spike, has stabilised but remains concentrated in specific geographic and demographic groups.

Cannabis at its highest ever recorded prevalence

At 12% past-year use, cannabis is more prevalent than in any previous NDSHS survey. Shifting attitudes — including ACT decriminalisation, growing medical cannabis access, and greater social acceptance — are all contributing. Young adults (18–34) account for a disproportionate share of users.

Chapter Four — Geographic Patterns

Remoteness & Risk

Drug use is not evenly distributed across Australia. Remoteness strongly predicts tobacco, cannabis and methamphetamine use, while cocaine is concentrated in major cities. The maps and charts below tell the story of two Australias: metropolitan areas where smoking is declining but vaping is emerging, and remote communities where tobacco remains a severe, under-resourced public health challenge.

20.4% Smoking rate in Remote/Very Remote areas

Two Australias: city and remote

In major cities, roughly 1 in 14 people smoke daily. In very remote communities, it is nearly 1 in 5. Cannabis and illicit drug use follow the same gradient. Vaping tells the opposite story — it is more prevalent in cities.

The map encodes this two-dimensional pattern: bubble size = rate, colour = remoteness category. Switch substances using the radio buttons.

Chapter Five — State Comparison

The NT Leads Every State on All Three Substances

Breaking substance use down by state reveals stark divides. The ACT consistently records the lowest rates. The NT records the highest across all three categories — smoking, vaping and drug use. The scatter plot reveals a positive correlation: states that smoke more also tend to use more drugs.
